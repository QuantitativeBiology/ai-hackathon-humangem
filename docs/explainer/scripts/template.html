Two fixes to Human1

How a published human metabolic model was made better at predicting which metabolic genes cancer cell lines need, what it still gets wrong, and how a team of AI agents arrived at it.

Given a cancer cell line's culture medium, P1 ranks 2,866 metabolic genes by how much simulated growth an edited Human1 model loses when each gene is knocked out, as a prediction of which genes the line needs.

  1. What it is. P1 is the published human metabolic model Human1 (a network of 2,887 genes and their reactions) with two changes: its growth reaction no longer demands a small pool of vitamin and cofactor derivatives, and it may take up only what the line's medium and serum supply, instead of a nucleoside-rich test medium.
  2. What it does. Flux balance analysis (FBA: the fluxes that maximise growth within uptake limits) knocks out each gene and records the growth lost; that score ranks the genes, with most of them tied at "no effect".
  3. How well. On 7 cancer tissues held out from all development, ranking accuracy against DepMap CRISPR screens rose from 0.297 to 0.347 (tie-aware AUPRC), positive in 7 of 7 tissues. The gain comes from removing errors shared by all lines; P1 adds no line-specific signal, and a gene-frequency prior with no model scores 0.895.

How it differs, and why that matters

Standard practice either knocks out genes in the generic model on a default medium (the Human1 and Human2 papers report Matthews correlation of about 0.25–0.45, usually the best of many settings and without whole-tissue hold-out), or builds a context-specific model per line from expression data (tINIT, GIMME, FASTCORE, E-Flux, CORDA). LITERATURE.md §0, §2.1, §3.1

P1 keeps the network and its gene rules as released and uses no omics. It changes what the model must achieve (the biomass reaction) and what it may take up (the medium). Three results make that matter:

  • The largest fixable error was in the objective. One biomass edit gained +0.032 on held-out lineages, while every expression-tailored family lost 0.003 to 0.013 on training lineages. shown results/w3/val/val_look_W3_M4_shortlist_decision.json; results/w4/stage_a_analysis/train_selection.json
  • The evaluation rewards real ranking. 94% of scores tie, so accuracy is tie-aware, on unseen tissues, next to cheap baselines that expose free gains (step 5).
  • The gain transferred without shrinking. P1 has no fitted parameters: +0.053 training, +0.052 validation, +0.050 test. results/m6/train/summary.json; results/m6/val/val_look_M6_factorial_decision.json; results/m6/test/test_look_T1_gates.json
Terms used on this page
  • Genome-scale metabolic model (GEM): the known metabolic reactions of human cells, each linked to its genes by a gene–protein–reaction (GPR) rule, a Boolean rule such as "subunit A AND subunit B" (complex) or "isozyme A OR isozyme B".
  • Human1: Human-GEM v1.19.0, 2,887 genes (Robinson et al. 2020). Human2: v2.1.1, 2,870 genes (Luo et al. 2026).
  • Biomass reaction: a pseudo-reaction (MAR13082) consuming a new cell's building blocks in fixed proportions; its flux is the model's "growth", in arbitrary units.
  • Knockout, called lethal: a gene's loss closes the reactions whose GPR becomes false; called lethal when growth falls below 1% (score e ≥ 0.99).
  • DepMap: CRISPR knockout screens across ~1,100 cancer lines; a gene is essential in a line when the dependency probability exceeds 0.5 (release 26Q3).
  • Lineage: tissue of origin; the unit held out. Common essentials: genes essential in nearly every line.
  • tAP: tie-aware average precision, the expected area under the precision–recall curve when tied scores are put in random order (McSherry & Najork 2008). Random ranking scores about 0.12 here.
  • Tiers: lines split once by lineage into training (12 lineages, 686 scored lines), validation (4, 198) and test (7, 380). Validation and test labels were sealed and read only through logged, capped evaluation runs (looks).
  • Reference: generic Human1 on Ham's medium (the Human-GEM "growth on Ham's" task, 44 inputs), fixed as comparator before any fix was scored.
  • Shown marks a claim measured in a committed result; believed marks interpretation; unverified marks what could not be checked.
1

Start from the generic Human1 network, unchanged

What
Load Human1 as released: no expression-based pruning, no edits to gene rules.
Why
  • Human1, not Human2. Human1 beat Human2 in all 12 training lineages (tAP 0.300 vs 0.292), on validation (−0.007 for Human2) and as P1's base on test (0.347 vs 0.341). The Human2 paper reports the opposite under its own protocol. results/w1/train_grid.tsv; results/w1/val_look_W1_M2_baseline_gstar.json; results/m6/test/unsealed/T1/w1_benchmark.tsv
  • No context-specific pruning. All five expression-based families and CORDA lost (−0.003 to −0.013), and less pruning was always better; on top of the biomass fix the best two added under +0.005, and E-Flux on full P1 added −0.0007. results/w4/stage_a_analysis/; results/m5/train/train_decision.json; results/f4/README.md
  • No GPR curation. Evidence-based curation lost 0.029; a stricter post-hoc rule still lost 0.0006. results/w5/train_summary.json; results/f1/README.md
Without it
Paralog merges turn true essentials into "viable" genes: merging RNA-polymerase subunits made 15 POLR genes non-lethal; merging RRM2 with RRM2B made RRM2, essential in 99.7% of test lines, non-lethal. Pruning removes correct generic calls faster than it adds line-specific ones believed.

2

Remove the cofactor pool from the biomass reaction

What

Human1's biomass reaction has 9 reactants. One is a pseudo-metabolite, the cofactor pool, built from 38 species: 20 retinoid catabolites, 4 vitamin-D catabolites, 2 tocopherol catabolites, carnitine, heme, ubiquinol, lipoate, cobamide and seven B-vitamin-type cofactors. P1 deletes that one term.

45 ATP + 45 H₂O + 5.3375 protein_pool + 0.4835 metabolite_pool + 0.4062 glycogen + 0.2212 lipid_pool + 0.1124 RNA + 0.0267 DNA + 0.0012 cofactor_pool (MAM10012c) → biomass

Reactants of MAR13082 as loaded by the worked example (data/worked_example.json). Normal growth barely changes: 65.573 → 65.577 a.u. on Ham's.

Why
  • With the pool in place, any gene needed to make one of the 38 species is lethal in every line, however small the coefficient. Several species are degradation products (retinoid and vitamin-D catabolites) a dividing cell has no reason to make. On Ham's, removal un-calls 35 genes.
  • Those 35 genes are essential in 14% of test lines on average (median 1.6%), against 73.5% for the genes P1 still calls lethal. This holds for the catabolite and carnitine genes (13%) and for the genes that make real cofactors such as heme and CoQ (15%). shown data/gene_groups.tsv from results/m6/test/unsealed/T1/per_gene.tsv
  • A model call that is the same in every line pays off only if the gene is essential in most lines, so un-calling both groups improves the ranking believed; the post-hoc decomposition puts their contribution at +0.033 of P1's +0.053 on training (62%). results/f2/decomposition.tsv
Without it
The medium fix alone gains +0.013 on test against +0.050 for P1. Removing the same number of calls at random lowers training tAP by 0.028, so the targeted removal beats random by +0.062. results/m6/test/unsealed/T1/attribution.tsv; notes/reviews/M4_W3_review.md
Known cost. The pool also holds ubiquinol, the mevalonate pathway's only non-sterol use. With the pool gone and serum supplying cholesterol (step 3), HMGCR, MVK, PMVK and MVD become non-lethal on all 19 serum media, although DepMap finds them essential in 43–90% of test lines. It costs P1 0.0014 on training. A safer variant that removes only catabolites and carnitine fixes it, at a validated cost of 0.021. results/m6/scores/w3_mev.tsv; results/f2/decomposition.tsv; results/m10/val/val_look_M10_F2_decision.json

3

Let the model take up only what the medium and serum supply

What
Uptake limits come from the line's documented base medium and supplements (RPMI-1640, DMEM, DMEM:F12, IMDM) in DepMap's annotations. Listed components are open; a micronutrient floor supplies vitamins the formulation omits; a serum model adds palmitate, stearate, oleate, cholesterol, carnitine and ethanolamine at the model's own requirement, plus iron, zinc and copper; cysteine stays closed unless 2-mercaptoethanol is present. Unknown media get classic RPMI-1640 + 10% FBS. nextgem.media.uptake_bounds (src/nextgem/media.py:727); results/w2/media_library/
Why
  • Ham's medium contains hypoxanthine and thymidine, which let the model bypass de novo purine and pyrimidine synthesis. On RPMI + serum, 8 genes become lethal (ATIC, GART, PAICS, PFAS, PPAT, TYMS, DHODH, UMPS); DepMap finds them essential in 44% of test lines on average.
  • Serum carnitine and cholesterol and the medium's glutathione rescue 13 calls essential in only 16% of test lines (BBOX1, TMLHE, ALDH9A1, DHCR7, GCLC, GCLM, GSS …). data/gene_groups.tsv
  • Medium-dependent metabolic essentiality is documented in DepMap and physiological-medium screens (Lagziel et al. 2019; Rossiter et al. 2021).
Without it
The biomass fix alone reaches +0.032 on validation, against +0.052 with both. Without serum the medium's training gain falls from +0.016 to +0.009. results/m6/val/val_look_M6_factorial_decision.json; results/w2/README.md
What it is not
It is not line-specific shown. A medium shuffled between lines of the same lineage and serum status keeps 97% of the gain; one constant RPMI + 10% FBS for every line scored slightly higher (+0.0015, post hoc); 1,050 of 1,361 lines share one classic-RPMI model, and 485 get it by default. In practice: a realistic medium with serum instead of Ham's. results/w2/train/shuffled_control.tsv; results/m6/scores/line_assignment.tsv

4

Knock out every gene and score the growth lost

What
Maximise growth for the unperturbed model; for each gene, close the reactions its loss disables, re-optimise, and record ratio = clip(round(KO / normal growth, 6), 0, 1); score e = 1 − ratio. Two exact shortcuts skip most solves: a gene whose reactions carry no flux cannot change growth, and genes closing the same reactions share one solve. One sweep: 170–215 linear programs, 30–45 CPU-s. nextgem.w3.ko_sweep (src/nextgem/w3.py:445); scripts/m6_sweeps.py
Why
Loss of biomass production is the standard FBA proxy for loss of proliferation. Growth-only calls beat the 57 "essential metabolic tasks" of the Human1 paper (task calls −0.015 on training; 30 of 57 single tasks lowered test tAP when added to P1). Rounding matters: unrounded ratios differ by float noise between identical runs, which moved AP by up to 0.018. results/w3/README.md; results/m6/test/unsealed/T1/per_task.tsv; notes/milestone_reviews/M1_process_review.md
Output
Nearly binary: for NCI-H460, 106 of 2,866 genes lethal, 64 partial, 2,696 (94%) tied at 0, only 17 distinct values.

5

Read the scores as a ranking with ties, against strong baselines

What
Per line, rank genes by e and score the ranking with tAP; average over lines with equal weight per lineage. Report it next to three cheap baselines: the gene prior (fraction of training lines in which the gene is essential; labels, no model), expression only, and the reference with ties broken by expression. nextgem.metrics.tap (src/nextgem/metrics.py:123)
Why
With 94% ties, ordinary AP depends on tie order: random tie-breaking alone moved AP by 0.025–0.053 per line in simulation. Breaking the reference's ties by expression is worth +0.12 (0.424 vs 0.300), more than both P1 fixes together. The gene prior reaches about 0.90 because DepMap essentiality is dominated by gene identity. notes/design_review_r1/statistics.md; results/w1/train_grid.tsv
Without it
A "better model" can be tie-breaking or gene identity in disguise. On test, P1 (0.347) beats the reference (0.297) but loses to the reference with an expression tie-break (0.418), and closes only 8.3% of the gap to the gene prior (0.895).

Worked example: NCI-H460, step by step

NCI-H460 (ACH-000463) is a large-cell lung carcinoma line in the training tier; DepMap documents its screen medium as "RPMI + 10% FBS". scripts/explainer_worked_example.py re-ran its four knockout sweeps with the project's own engine; every score equals the committed P1 table (maximum difference 0), in 2.6 CPU-min. No label file is read: the line's accuracy comes from the committed per-line table, and instead of the line's own labels each gene shows how often DepMap finds it essential across the 380 test lines. results/m6/train/per_line.tsv; results/m6/test/unsealed/T1/per_gene.tsv

Pseudocode and code

# P1: per-line metabolic gene-essentiality scores. Label-free; no fitted parameters.
model = load_model("human1")                                  # step 1  nextgem.model.load_model   (src/nextgem/model.py:50)
edit  = Variant(kind="remove", mets=["MAM10012c"])            # step 2  nextgem.w3.Variant         (src/nextgem/w3.py:378)

for line in lines:                                            # step 3  nextgem.media.assign_line_media (media.py:793)
    medium = documented base medium + serum, else classic RPMI + 10% FBS
    bounds[line] = uptake_bounds(model, "M2", medium, floor, f=1.0)   #  media.py:727

for b in unique(bounds.values()):                             # step 4  nextgem.w3.ko_sweep (w3.py:445)
    with model:
        model.medium = b; edit.apply(model); objective = "MAR13082"
        wt = optimise(model)
        for gene in genes:
            ratio = 1 if gene's closed reactions carry no flux in wt     # exact shortcut
                    else optimise(model with gene knocked out) / wt     # one LP per distinct closed set
            e[b, gene] = 1 - clip(round(ratio, 6), 0, 1)

score[line, gene] = e[bounds[line], gene]                     # gene absent from Human1 -> 0
# evaluation only: tAP per line (metrics.tap, metrics.py:123); lineage-equal mean (:526); line bootstrap (:636)

Drivers: scripts/m6_sweeps.py (sweeps), scripts/m6_scores.py (score tables), scripts/evaluate_val.py and scripts/evaluate_test.py (frozen evaluation harnesses). Ready-made models: 48 SBML files, one per parent model and medium, mapped to 2,114 DepMap lines; nextgem.resource.load_line("resource", "<ModelID>", "human1") (src/nextgem/resource.py:519). 47 of 48 pass all export gates (the miss is LP noise of 8e-9).

Limitations and when not to use P1

Flags carried by every P1 claim

  1. The biomass fix's negative control was settled after training results were seen; a later random-removal control (+0.062), validation and test support it (turning point 5).
  2. The mevalonate core is non-lethal under serum (cost 0.0014 on training).
  3. The documented medium is no better than a shuffled one.
  4. Opening every uptake also scores well on all genes (+0.020) but −0.017 without common essentials.

Structural limits and assumptions

  • Recall ceiling: about 45% of essential gene × line pairs involve genes whose knockout changes nothing in Human1. results/w1/train_addressable.tsv
  • Real cofactor requirements are removed with the pool; DepMap rarely finds them essential, which is why it helps the score. Whether the biology is right is not shown.
  • Uptake limits mean "available", not a rate; growth is in arbitrary units.
  • Assumes loss of biomass flux approximates loss of fitness in a pooled screen, and that the screen medium (or classic RPMI) describes the culture.

Not validated

The biomass fix alone on test; P1 with an expression tie-break; per-line medium effects; lineage or genomic-background group models; quantitative growth or flux; synthetic lethality (double knockouts, named as a goal in the README but not analysed); patient tumours; other DepMap releases or models.

When not to use it

  • For line-specific predictions: expression ranks lines better (turning point 7).
  • When labels or a gene prior are available: the prior (0.895) and the gene-prior ML model (≈ 0.90) are far more accurate (turning point 8).
  • For sterol and mevalonate, heme, CoQ or B-vitamin cofactor genes.
  • When expression is available and only ranking matters: try the expression tie-break first.
  • As a "personalised model" resource: the 48 models differ only by medium.

How we got here

P1 is simple because nearly every ambitious idea failed a fair test, while one small structural fix survived every test.

The brief asked for context-specific models built from transcriptomics, proteomics and genomics. What worked was fixing two errors every line shares. Three things decided the method's shape:

  • The evaluation came first. Tie-aware, on unseen tissues, against a gene-frequency prior: it showed how little room the model had and exposed gains that were only tie-breaking (turning point 1, turning point 2).
  • The data-driven levers failed. Expression-tailored models and GPR curation lost accuracy (turning point 3, turning point 4).
  • The fixes that worked target shared errors. Clues found while debugging those failures pointed at the biomass reaction (turning point 5); the medium helped for generic reasons (turning point 6); the combination held on test (turning point 7); negatives set the limits (turning point 8); post-hoc follow-ups confirmed the design (epilogue).

The work was done by a main agent acting as principal investigator (PI) and ~60 subagents over about 2.3 days. Times are UTC; day 1 is 26 October 2026.

The research process

Who did what

The main agent (PI) wrote and froze the design, took every decision (D0–D19; amendments A1–A15), made every commit and ran every look. Subagents: 56 before this explainer (42 Opus, 14 Sonnet) plus four follow-up engineers. Roughly half built (workstream engineers, harness implementers, the report drafter) and half checked (seven design reviewers, an unexposed verifier, a blind re-deriver of the a-priori gene lists, code reviewers, non-author recomputers of every result relied on, four process reviewers). The split is from task descriptions unverified.

What was productive

  • Independent recomputation caught a float-tie AP instability (0.018), three defects that would have invalidated the 108-CPU-h context-model run, a reversed candidate order, and two test-harness bugs before the test look.
  • The reviewer who distrusted the biomass result supplied the random-removal control and found the mevalonate cost (turning point 5).
  • The winning idea came from label-free structural observation made while debugging other failures.
  • Pre-registration kept claims small: the line-specificity test failed and was reported as failed.

Where it went in circles

  • Review and fix rounds: about 40% compute, 40% review and fixes, 20% amendments and bookkeeping in mid-project (21k words of review for ~135 CPU-h). The design took three review rounds and 51 decisions. notes/milestone_reviews/M3M4_process_review.md
  • Harness extensions, each with a full code review; ~3 h of write–review–fix for the test harness.
  • After-the-fact rulings (the biomass control reading; a medium eligible set not provably fixed in advance) and procedure amendments despite a rule against them.

Stalls not recorded in the repository

Usage limits on day 1 22:48–00:20, day 2 12:22–19:00 (weekly; it delayed the biomass result by ~6.6 h), day 2 23:52–00:00 and day 3 03:54–05:00, about 9 h; plus a 4.4-h wait for a user answer on day 2 (03:15–07:41). Together ~0.55 day of a ~2.3-day project against a 1–1.5-day target; the process reviews attributed the overrun to review rounds only.

Incidents

An exported PYTHONPATH aborted the first test rehearsal at its ledger commit (no look consumed); a wrapper's exit status was once read as the build's; a commit said "all gates pass" for 39 of 40; a reviewer left a label copy in scratch (deleted) and another ran the real split seed (retired); two workstreams ran from uncommitted code (reproduced bitwise); ~15 CPU-h lost to a solver stall and a BLAS-thread incident.

Key insights

Ranked by how general, then how surprising. Each links to where it arose.

Scientific

  1. S1The biggest fixable error was the biomass reaction, not missing cell-line context. A cofactor pool at coefficient 0.0012 makes every gene that synthesises it "lethal" in every line; removing it gained +0.032 on held-out lineages, while no data-integration method gained anything. Arose in turning point 5 · step 2
  2. S2A line-constant call is a bet on a gene's frequency, and the biomass made bad bets. The 35 genes the pool forced lethal are essential in only 14% of test lines, whether they make catabolites (13%) or real cofactors such as heme and CoQ (15%); P1's remaining lethal genes average 73.5%. Arose in follow-ups · step 2
  3. S3Most DepMap metabolic essentiality is gene identity, not cell-line metabolism. A gene prior scores 0.90 against 0.30; P1 closes 8% of the gap; ~45% of essential pairs are invisible to FBA; no line-specific signal beyond expression. Arose in turning point 2 · turning point 7

Methodological

  1. M1With near-binary predictors, tie handling can outweigh the model. 92–94% of genes tie; random tie order moves AP by 0.025–0.053 per line, and an expression tie-break is worth +0.12, more than both P1 fixes. Arose in turning point 1 · step 5
  2. M2Test "context" against shuffled and constant counterfactuals. A shuffled medium kept 97% of the documented medium's gain and a constant one did slightly better, turning "line-specific medium" into "realistic medium". Arose in turning point 6
  3. M3Broad screens need held-out confirmation and controls fixed before looking. Over 134 modes the best-of-many inflation (0.049) exceeded every training effect; only a validation look separated the real fix, and its control had to be repaired after the fact. Arose in turning point 5

Scientific (the rest)

  1. S4The medium matters generically, not per line. Ham's nucleosides hide 8 de novo nucleotide genes (essential in 44% of lines); serum rescues 13 calls essential in 16%; physiological media did no better than the classic media screens used. turning point 6 · step 3
  2. S5The remaining errors are outside the model's scope. All 20 worst test errors are housekeeping essentials (translation, glycosylation, ubiquitination, mRNA capping, DNA replication) that Human1 does not represent or hides behind lumped OR rules. F3
  3. S6A newer, more curated model was not better here. Human2 was below Human1 on every tier, under a different protocol from its paper's. turning point 2
  4. S7Paralog OR rules are dangerous near essential reactions. RNA-polymerase merges cost 0.029; even a strict rule merged RRM2 (99.7% essential) with RRM2B (0%). turning point 4 · F1
  5. S8Biomass pseudo-reactants couple pathways in hidden ways. Removing the pool removes ubiquinol and with it mevalonate essentiality under serum; a vitamin-D demand blocks the cholesterol rescue of sterol genes; a dihydroorotate entry blocks the uridine rescue of CAD. turning point 5
  6. S9Line-specific constraints barely move FBA knockouts. Genomic losses in 665 lines changed 22 calls; flux from models that differ only by medium carries no line information. turning point 8 · F4
  7. S10GEM outputs add nothing to gene-prior machine learning (−0.003; stacking +0.000). turning point 8

Methodological (the rest)

  1. M4Read every result against the strongest cheap baseline. P1 beats the reference by +0.050 but trails the expression tie-break by 0.071 and the gene prior by 0.548. turning point 2 · turning point 7
  2. M5Decompose a gain by gene group before interpreting it. "Removing artefacts" was the story; un-calling real-cofactor genes carried much of the gain, and the known mevalonate error cost only 0.0014. F2
  3. M6Parameter-free fixes transfer; CI width does not tell you how far. +0.053 / +0.052 / +0.050 across tiers; transfer rests on 7/7 lineages. turning point 7
  4. M7A pre-registered order of tests stops overclaiming. The failed line-specificity test blocked any personalisation claim. turning point 7
  5. M8Non-author recomputation catches what authors miss. process
  6. M9Watch for runner-up shrinkage: an ML lead of +0.014 on training was +0.0007 on validation. turning point 8
  7. M10Clues found while debugging failures can beat the planned experiment. turning point 5

Discrepancies between documents and results
  1. "Biomass artefacts" overstates the edit: README.md lists BH4 and CoQ among false calls; the F2 follow-up treats them as real cofactors, and un-calling those 20 genes carries much of the gain.
  2. Wall-time accounts omit ~13 h of stalls found in the transcript.
  3. REPORT.md drifted at HEAD: looks 6 → 7, runs 152 → 174, CPU-h 201.2 → 210.4, wall-h 28.4 → 30.0, elapsed 42.9 → 46.1.
  4. Stale status: RESEARCH_TREE.md summary says F2 and F4 "in progress"; results/f2/README.md says the look was "not run" (it ran, look 7); README.md says review "in progress" and cites A1–A14; REPORT.md §11 lists as owed what F2–F4 delivered.
  5. The factorial cells are numbered two ways (medium-first in the tree and code; biomass-first in REPORT.md §4.7 and the training summary).
  6. "Documented medium" overstates line specificity: 36% of lines get classic RPMI by default.
  7. The README's disclaimer names synthetic lethality as a goal; no analysis addresses it.
  8. Minor: the headline p written three ways; two bootstrap CIs for the same contrast; the line-specificity CI excludes its point estimate; Human1 lethal count 149 vs 147 (gene universe); W3 runtime 24.5 vs 20.9 CPU-h; citation gaps (CORDA, Machado, McSherry).
Open questions

  3. The biomass fix alone was never scored on test.
  4. Lineage and genomic-background group models were never evaluated.
  5. Why are SPCS2/SPCS3 (essential in 93–94% of lines) un-called by the pool removal and by serum?
